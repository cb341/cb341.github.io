reflecting over the past semesters at ZHAW. exams keep frustrating me and i'm
still working out why.

**hypothesis**

when i started, i quietly assumed grades were a decent proxy for understanding.

high grade → strong understanding.
low grade → weak understanding.
strong students → the ones with the best mental models.

clean. i didn't question it much.

**observation**

then it stopped holding.

i did well in subjects where i didn't feel i understood the core particularly
deeply. i hit exams that rewarded executing a procedure more than actually
transferring an idea to a new shape. i met students with weaker grades asking the
more interesting questions, and students with stronger grades who understood the
material and executed the format.

and i caught the same pattern in myself.

if pure exam score had been the goal, i'd have studied differently. less time on
alternative derivations, questioning assumptions, playing with formulas, chasing
tangents. more time drilling exercise schemas until pattern recognition went
automatic.

that strategy works, for the grade. i let curiosity pull me off what was asked
instead. the drift was mine, not the format's.

**updated model**

so grades aren't meaningless. they measure *something*.

just a narrower something than i thought.

they're a snapshot of performance under one assessment format. understanding,
transfer, curiosity, judgment, model quality ; only partially visible through
that lens.

**conclusion**

i wish universities rewarded curiosity directly. transfer, reasoning, the ability
to build durable models of the world.

but i get why they don't.

curiosity is hard to grade. you can't really put two people's reasoning side by
side and rank them, and from the outside it looks a lot like just being good at
the test. so they fall back on something they can count. and a university with
thousands of students kind of has to. it's not going to rebuild itself around
what i happen to want.

so the more interesting question isn't how universities *should* change.

it's what i do with knowing this.

**if grades are partial signals, treat them as partial signals.**

if an exam optimizes for assessment performance, don't confuse assessment
performance with understanding. and if the things i value most are only weakly
measured by the institution, then cultivating them is on me.

not because they're rewarded. reading an RFC, following a tangent off an exam
topic until it makes sense ; the grade doesn't see any of that.

grades were the direction i started in. probably a partial one.

the new goal is becoming less wrong. and i think the test for that isn't the exam
; it's whether i can teach the thing, explain it from scratch, actually apply it
somewhere real. if i can't, the grade didn't mean what i thought it did.

**observation**

then it got more complicated.

some exams perturb the model:

* derive from assumptions
* choose the method yourself
* state why it applies
* trace code
* find the errors
* explain the meaning
* handle changed conditions

others are procedural drills:

* integrate this form
* use this substitution
* apply this derivation
* follow this procedure
* use these parameters
* answer the obvious true/false

i respect the first kind more. and i scored higher on the second.

that's the uncomfortable part. the exams that tested the thing i claim to value
are the ones i underperformed on, and i'd been blaming the format for it.

if pure exam score had been the goal, i'd have studied differently. less time on
alternative derivations, questioning assumptions, playing with formulas, chasing
tangents. more time drilling exercise schemas until pattern recognition went
automatic.

that strategy works, for the grade. i let curiosity pull me off what each exam
actually asked instead. the drift was mine, not the format's.
